\section*{Abstract}
Although it is generally thought that the formation of cognitive maps requires the brain to integrate self-movement (i.e., path integration), recent studies challenge this view by showing the existence of spatial selectivity in deep networks trained with vision inputs alone.
Because these studies only examined neuronal selectivity to individual spatial variables (e.g., location, head direction), without characterizing their joint tuning,
it remains possible that the ways in which space is encoded in the brain and in these models are substantially different.
We develop a framework for estimating conditional response variation and use it to compare selectivity and invariance in biological and artificial neural populations. Neurons in the brain and in the models show qualitatively different selectivity and invariance to location and head direction. Our results demonstrate the importance of studying joint tuning properties in understanding neural codes.

\section{Introduction}

Examining the tuning property of individual neurons is a basic approach for studying the neural code~\cite{Hubel1959,o1971,Hafting2005,Deangelis1991,decharms2000neural,Stensola2012,Kriegeskorte2021}. Over the past few decades, neuroscientists have characterized the tuning properties of neurons to behavioral variables in many brain regions~(e.g., \cite{Hafting2005,Deangelis1991,decharms2000neural,Stensola2012,Taube1990}).
In the hippocampus and related brain areas (e.g., entorhinal cortex and parahippocampus), neurons selective to various spatial variables (e.g., location, head direction, speed) have been discovered~\cite{o1971,o1978,Hafting2005,Stensola2012,Kropff2015speed,Taube1990}. Earlier studies focused mainly on the selectivity to one stimulus variable (e.g., visual orientation, location, or head direction). More recent studies also examined the selectivity of neurons to multiple stimulus dimensions individually. If a neuron is selective to multiple variables, the neuron is considered to exhibit conjunctive tuning~\cite{Sargolini2006conjunctive} or mixed selectivity~\cite{Rigotti2013,Fusi2016neurons,Hardcastle2017multiplexed}.

A large body of work has investigated how the brain constructs spatial maps of the environment. This question is relevant both for understanding how the brain supports navigation~\cite{Mcnaughton2006} and for building embodied intelligent agents~\cite{Cadena2017}.
It is generally believed that the brain integrates self-movement signals (i.e., velocity input and head rotation) to generate such maps~\cite{Mcnaughton2006} (the path integration hypothesis~\cite{Darwin1873}). Other sensory inputs, such as visual landmarks and boundaries, are thought to recalibrate the cognitive map~\cite{Hardcastle2015environmental,Zhang1996}. Recurrent neural network models, either hand-crafted~\cite{Samsonovich1997,Zhang1996,Burak2009} or optimized for navigation tasks~\cite{Cueva2018,Banino2018,Sorscher2020,Cueva2020,Gao2021}, have been developed to model place cells, head direction cells, and grid cells.
Recent studies challenged this view. In deep neural network models trained to solve purely visual tasks, many network units exhibit spatial selectivity that resembles that observed in the rodent hippocampus~\cite{Gornet2024automated,Luo2024}.
These studies compared marginal tuning curves, which alone do not establish whether responses are preserved as other stimulus or behavioral variables change.

Understanding a neural representation requires knowing not only which variables influence activity, but also whether tuning to one variable is preserved as other stimulus or behavioral variables change. Such preservation can support consistent readout by downstream circuits. This distinction matters when comparing representations, particularly across artificial and biological systems. We quantify this property by measuring conditional response variation, including changes in tuning shape, gain, and baseline.

We apply this framework to spatial and directional representations in biological neurons and artificial neural networks. Recent reports of place-like and head-direction-like units in networks trained on visual tasks have motivated comparisons with biological cell types~\cite{Luo2024,Gornet2024automated}. However, similar marginal tuning curves need not imply similar patterns of selectivity and invariance. Our analysis reveals a marked distinction between biological and artificial representations. CA1 place cells are selective to location and largely invariant to head direction, while postsubicular head direction cells are selective to head direction and largely invariant to location. In contrast, units in the examined artificial networks exhibit entangled tuning to both variables.

We then apply the framework to a related question in biological navigation: whether head direction cells also encode movement direction, a signal required by path-integration models. Because head direction and movement direction are behaviorally correlated, marginal tuning alone cannot resolve their contributions. We find that head direction cells are largely invariant to movement direction, with implications for how downstream navigation circuits obtain movement-related signals.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Marginal tuning curves are insufficient to capture the joint tuning}

When only one stimulus variable (denoted $x$, such as the head direction of the animal) is considered, the tuning property of a neuron can be described by its tuning curve $f(x)$, which quantifies how the firing rate of the neuron varies with the stimulus variable.
Suppose that another stimulus variable $y$ is also of interest (e.g., the movement direction of the animal), and that our goal is to understand whether this neuron is selective to both $x$ and $y$. The standard approach is to examine the marginal tuning curves $f(x)$ and $g(y)$ separately to see whether one or both exhibit strong selectivity. If $f(x)$ is a narrow Gaussian function and $g(y)$ is flat, it is tempting to conclude that the neuron is selective to $x$ and invariant to $y$. However, this analysis of the marginal tuning curves can misrepresent the joint tuning to the two variables.

Generalized linear models (GLMs) offer a way to identify variables that predict neural responses~\cite{Pillow2008spatio,Hardcastle2017multiplexed}, but the joint tuning they can describe depends on the model's terms and link function. When the log firing rate is a sum of separate functions of each variable, the firing rate is a product of those functions after exponentiation. Such a model allows one variable to rescale tuning to another, but cannot change its shape or preferred value. Interaction terms allow these additional changes.

Fig.~\ref{fig:glm} illustrates this distinction with two simulated neurons. A Poisson GLM with separate quadratic terms for $x$ and $y$ approximates the marginal tuning but misses the orientation of the joint tuning and its conditional slices. Adding an $xy$ interaction recovers these features and improves held-out likelihood. Agreement with marginal tuning curves therefore does not establish whether interaction terms are needed, and variable selection restricted to additive models cannot assess their contribution.

Correlated sampling of the stimuli further complicates the interpretation of marginal tuning curves. Correlated stimulus variables are common. In spatial navigation, one notable example is the animal's head direction and movement direction~\cite{Raudies2015head}; another is time and distance traveled when the animal runs on a linear track. Suppose that a neuron is selective to $x$ and completely invariant to $y$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}). Because of the correlation in stimulus sampling, the marginal tuning curve for $y$ still shows substantial selectivity (see Methods). Relying on the marginal tuning curves alone, we would incorrectly conclude that the neuron is tuned to $y$, although its response is completely invariant to $y$. Examining joint tuning can avoid these issues.

\subsection{A method for analyzing the invariance and selectivity of neurons}

The results above show that analyzing neural selectivity and invariance requires examining joint tuning beyond marginal tuning curves. How should we quantify the contribution of an individual stimulus variable to a neuron's activity? A simple starting point is to vary a stimulus variable $x$ and examine tuning to the remaining variables. With two variables, this amounts to examining the tuning curve for $y$ conditioned on different values of $x$. The response is invariant to $x$ if this conditional tuning curve remains unchanged across values of $x$. Changes in its shape, gain, or baseline indicate that the response depends on $x$.

To quantify the contribution of each variable while accounting for nonlinear interactions, we measure the variability in the joint tuning map that is not explained by the other variables. With two variables, the contribution of $x$ is the response variance across $x$ at each fixed $y$, averaged over $y$. This is the expected conditional variance: the response variability remaining after accounting for $y$ alone. The contribution of $y$ is computed symmetrically. Such conditional-variance measures have previously been used to characterize position and orientation selectivity in visual-learning models~\cite{Franzius2007}.

Measurement noise complicates the estimation of these contributions. Consider a neuron whose response is selective to $x$ and completely invariant to $y$. Noise introduces variability in the estimated joint tuning map that cannot be explained by $x$. Using this residual variability directly would therefore overestimate the contribution of $y$. To correct for this effect, we estimate the joint tuning map separately from two halves of the data, center each map over the variable of interest, and average the cross-fold products (Fig.~\ref{fig:estimation}). With unbiased fold estimates, independent zero-mean errors, and fixed support and weights, this estimates the conditional signal variance without additive noise bias (Methods).

We normalize both contributions by the same cross-fold estimate of total signal variance to compare neurons with different response scales. The normalized ratios are not themselves guaranteed to be unbiased. Interactions contribute to both conditional variances, so the normalized scores are not exclusive fractions that must sum to one.

Simulations recover the known conditional variances of the binned response maps (Fig.~\ref{fig:estimation}). Within-bin sampling differences can nevertheless produce apparent dependence on an irrelevant variable. Smoothing reduces this effect in our simulation (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:binning_bias}); we examine its implications for resolution choice below.

\subsection{Model units in deep network models based on vision exhibit entangled spatial tuning}

While many studies suggest that path integration is important for the formation of cognitive maps in the brain~\cite{Mcnaughton2006,Burak2009,Cueva2018}, recent studies reported place-cell-like responses in deep neural networks trained or pretrained to perform vision tasks alone~\cite{Luo2024,Gornet2024automated}. Gornet and Thomson~\cite{Gornet2024automated} reported that training a virtual navigation agent to predict the next image frame led to the formation of cognitive maps, with units in certain hidden layers selective to the agent's location, similar to place cells. Luo et al.~\cite{Luo2024} analyzed the responses of units in ResNet (pretrained on image classification, or untrained) in a spatial environment and reported that a large fraction of units throughout the network exhibit spatial selectivity to location or head direction. These studies~\cite{Luo2024,Gornet2024automated} examined the marginal tuning to these spatial variables separately, without examining their joint tuning.
We examine the joint tuning of units in the types of deep network models studied in~\cite{Luo2024,Gornet2024automated}, and then compare them with neurons in the rodent spatial navigation system.

{\bf Model units in networks trained on predictive coding exhibit entangled spatial tuning}
Inspired by prior work on predictive coding~\cite{Srinivasan1982predictive,Rao1999predictive}, Gornet and Thomson~\cite{Gornet2024automated} trained a virtual navigation agent, implemented as a deep neural network, to predict the next image frame in a sequence of images as the agent moves in space and found place-cell-like responses in its intermediate layers.

We trained this model on image observations collected along smooth trajectories in Minecraft, with the agent's head direction matching its direction of movement. For the retained checkpoint, selected by validation error, next-frame mean-squared error on held-out test trajectories was $70.0\%$ lower than that obtained by copying the current frame (Methods). We focused on the latent representations entering and leaving the self-attention prediction module: the encoder's representation of the current observation and the predicted representation used to generate the next observation. Gornet and Thomson interpret the latent representation as a spatial code that the self-attention module transforms to predict subsequent observations~\cite{Gornet2024automated}. We therefore examine this representation before and after the prediction step. For comparison with the convolutional representations examined in the models of Luo et al.~\cite{Luo2024}, we also examined two earlier encoder layers, the first convolutional layer and the first downsampling block. We evaluated each unit across the sampled combinations of 2-D location and head direction.
Using established place-field criteria~\cite{Tanni2022}, a fraction of units in the intermediate layers are classified as putative place cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:pred}), consistent with~\cite{Gornet2024automated}. The joint tuning to location and head direction of these units is complex and entangled (Fig.~\ref{fig:pred}). When conditioned on different head directions, the location tuning maps change (Fig.~\ref{fig:pred}): the location selectivity of these units is not invariant to head direction.

Quantifying conditional response variation reveals substantial dependence on both location and head direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:pred}; pre-prediction layer: median $\mathrm{EV}_{xy}=0.93$ and $\mathrm{EV}_{hd}=0.92$; $7{,}755$ of $8{,}000$ units have defined normalized scores, with location larger in $53.1\%$). Units classified exclusively as putative place cells also depend substantially on heading (Fig.~\ref{fig:pred}; median location/HD scores of $0.93/0.91$ before prediction, $n=6{,}713$, and $0.93/0.91$ after prediction, $n=6{,}883$). Substantial heading dependence remains when we additionally require consistency between maps averaged over two heading groups (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ann_stability}). The main figure shows both latent layers, with place-classified units highlighted in red; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:si_pred_ev} extends the analysis to all four examined layers.

{\bf Pretrained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) also exhibit entangled spatial tuning}  We next investigate deep neural networks pretrained to perform image classification. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are a popular modeling framework for how the brain processes visual information~\cite{Yamins2014,Khaligh2014,Yamins2016}. Luo et al.~\cite{Luo2024} proposed using CNNs to study the navigation system in the brain. They modeled the visual inputs of a virtual agent navigating a 2-D open arena similar to the environments of rodent neurophysiology experiments. These CNNs are pretrained on visual tasks and receive only visual inputs, with no explicit input about the agent's velocity. Luo et al.~\cite{Luo2024} reported that many units throughout the network exhibit place-cell-like responses as well as head direction tuning.

We analyzed a ResNet-50~\cite{He2016} pretrained on image classification in the same Minecraft environment used for the predictive coding network, which is similar to the environment of~\cite{Luo2024}. Consistent with~\cite{Luo2024}, a fraction of units exhibit putative place fields in 2-D space. Luo et al.~\cite{Luo2024} also reported that many units exhibit strong head direction tuning and that a subset exhibit conjunctive place and HD tuning. In our analysis of marginal tuning, few units are tuned to HD, and consequently few units are tuned to both HD and location. In the code released with~\cite{Luo2024}, the criterion for head direction cells does not properly capture selectivity to head direction: applying that criterion to our network also classifies many units as head direction cells. Applying the alternative criterion to the same responses changes the prevalence of HD classifications, demonstrating sensitivity to the classification rule. This within-data comparison does not isolate every difference between studies.

The joint location$\times$HD maps of units classified as putative place cells show firing fields scattered through the 3-D space (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:si_scattered_maps}): a unit is active only for particular combinations of location and HD, as in the network trained on next-frame prediction. Across the analyzed trained layers, both location and head direction account for substantial conditional response variation (Fig.~\ref{fig:CNN} for four layers of ResNet-50; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:si_cnn_ev} for VGG-16~\cite{Simonyan2015} and an untrained ResNet-50). Median HD scores for the exclusive place-classified subsets range from $0.89$ to $0.94$ across the four pretrained ResNet-50 layers. The field criteria also classify many units in the untrained network: $1{,}968$ of $2{,}048$ units in its pooled output layer meet the exclusive place criterion, with median location/HD scores of $0.86/0.78$.

Together, these results reproduce key findings of recent studies of spatial tuning in deep neural networks trained on purely visual tasks: in the marginal tuning to 2-D location, a fraction of units have putative place fields. The joint tuning, however, shows that location and head direction are strongly entangled in these models. The trained-model population medians are high on both axes, in contrast to the opposing population preferences observed in the biological datasets. The untrained ResNet control shows a larger location preference while retaining substantial HD dependence.

\subsection{Distinct selectivity and invariance of place cells and head direction cells in rodent brain}

We next analyze neurons recorded in the rodent spatial navigation system and compare them to the deep network models. We analyze two datasets: CA1 place cells recorded while rats navigated a 2-D open arena~\cite{Pfeiffer2013}, and head direction cells recorded from the postsubiculum of mice~\cite{Duszkiewicz2024}. Beyond the selectivity that defines each cell type, we ask whether these cells are invariant to the other spatial variables, e.g., whether place cells are invariant to head direction.

\textbf{CA1 place cells depend more strongly on location than on head direction} We analyzed $545$ CA1 neurons from four rats that met the published place-cell criterion of Pfeiffer and Foster~\cite{Pfeiffer2013}: excitatory classification and a peak spatial firing rate above $1$~Hz (Methods). Consistent with previous reports, many of these neurons are selective to location in the open arena, with compact place fields, and most do not exhibit strong marginal tuning to head direction.
As shown above, marginal tuning alone does not establish invariance to another variable. We therefore computed the joint tuning map for location and HD, a 3-D map for each neuron (Fig.~\ref{fig:PlaceCell}). Visual inspection suggests that the spatial response is largely preserved across head directions: the example cells retain localized place fields across the heading sectors shown. Firing rates nevertheless vary across sectors, indicating some modulation by head direction.

We quantified this pattern using the conditional response-variance analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:PlaceCell}). Response variation was substantially greater for location than for head direction: location had the larger score in $98.1\%$ of cells, with median location and HD scores of $0.75$ and $0.08$, respectively.

The CA1 populations decode location with a pooled held-out median error of $11.2$~cm, compared with $81.3$~cm for the occupancy-based baseline. Head direction is decoded better than the occupancy-based baseline, although the median angular error remains large ($54.6\degree$ versus $79.3\degree$). All four rats improve on both variables. Thus the weaker single-cell HD response dependence does not imply an absence of population HD information. Including all $551$ supplied excitatory cells leaves the normalized EV results unchanged and yields nearly identical decoding errors (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ca1_selection}).

\textbf{Postsubicular HD cells are selective to head direction and largely invariant to location} Visual inspection of the joint tuning maps shows directional tuning across different locations, with some spatial modulation. We quantified this pattern in $1{,}489$ excitatory HD cells from 30 sessions~\cite{Duszkiewicz2024}. HD had the larger conditional response-variance score in $96.9\%$ of cells, with median HD and location scores of $0.80$ and $0.29$, respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:HD}).

Head direction is decoded with a pooled median error of $8.7\degree$, versus $81.3\degree$ for the occupancy-based baseline. Location is decoded slightly better than the occupancy baseline ($33.4$ versus $43.8$~cm; 28 of 30 sessions improve).

Together, these results provide evidence of a double dissociation in conditional response dependence: location dependence dominates in CA1 place cells, whereas head-direction dependence dominates in postsubicular HD cells. The contrast persists without interpolation of the location$\times$HD maps. Its magnitude depends on spatial resolution, particularly for the HD-cell population (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:binning_sensitivity}).

{\bf Comparison of the deep network models and the rodent hippocampus} The joint tuning to head direction and location reveals a large discrepancy between model units and spatial cells in the brain. Model units exhibit comparable conditional response dependence on head direction and location, whereas CA1 and HD-cell populations show opposing preferences. These models based on visual inputs alone therefore do not account for the key properties of the spatial cells found in the brain, and the joint tuning of multiple variables provides a more informative signature than marginal tuning for testing such models.

\subsection{Head direction cells are only weakly tuned to movement direction, limiting their potential contribution to path integration}

The analysis of joint tuning may have broad relevance for testing computational theories of brain function. We next use it to study a question that is central to understanding grid cells~\cite{Hafting2005,Stensola2012}. A popular class of computational models proposes that the hexagonal grid firing pattern results from path integration~\cite{Burak2009,Mcnaughton2006,Couey2013,Cueva2018}. This class of models requires that grid cells receive input about the animal's velocity (i.e., movement direction and speed). Neurons that encode movement speed have been identified in the entorhinal cortex and other areas~\cite{Kropff2015speed,Hinman2016}, but neurons that selectively encode movement direction have not been identified, and head direction is often assumed to stand in for movement direction.
Although the two are correlated, a previous study~\cite{Raudies2015head} found that (i) head direction is more strongly encoded in head direction cells than movement direction, and (ii) head direction and movement direction are sufficiently distinct that head direction would be an insufficient input for generating grid patterns in path integration models. It has remained unclear whether head direction cells are tuned to movement direction (MD) once the correlation between head and movement directions is taken into account, and whether the head direction population encodes a movement direction signal accurate enough for path integration.

We re-analyzed the head direction cells of~\cite{Duszkiewicz2024} to address these questions. A substantial fraction of these cells show marginal tuning to movement direction. Because movement direction and head direction are correlated, this observation alone does not establish whether responses depend on movement direction beyond their dependence on head direction. We therefore examined their joint tuning. HD and MD lie within $30\degree$ of one another in $39.85\%$ of eligible time bins, compared with $17.02\%$ under independence of the empirical angular marginals (Fig.~\ref{fig:MD}). Across the entire population, movement direction accounted for only a small component of conditional response variation: its median normalized contribution was $0.05$, compared with $0.90$ for head direction, and the contribution of head direction was larger in every cell. Finer $4\degree$ binning, movement-estimation windows, and speed thresholds are examined in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:md_sensitivity}.

On matched time bins with speed at least 5~cm/s, MD decoding is substantially less accurate than HD decoding: pooled median errors are $48.4\degree$ for MD and $8.4\degree$ for HD, compared with respective occupancy-based baselines of $89.9\degree$ and $83.3\degree$. MD is nevertheless decoded better than its baseline in all 30 sessions; its accuracy improves when the analysis is restricted to faster movement. Because HD and MD are behaviorally correlated, above-baseline MD decoding alone does not establish response dependence on MD beyond HD. These results suggest that the MD signal in the head direction cells is weak and unlikely to be sufficient for accurate path integration.

\section{Discussion}

We demonstrate that joint tuning to multiple stimulus variables contains critical information that marginal tuning curves alone do not reveal. In particular, selectivity and invariance need to be studied together to characterize the neural code.
GLM analyses restricted to separate terms for each variable impose a particular structure on joint tuning and can miss dependencies that require interactions. More generally, identifying the variables that predict a neuron's response does not establish how its tuning is preserved as other variables change. Our analysis directly quantifies this aspect of the representation through conditional response variation. A model without interactions in its log firing rate can still produce gain modulation, so measuring response invariance is distinct from testing whether such interactions improve prediction. Hardcastle et al.~\cite{Hardcastle2017multiplexed} compared gain scaling with baseline shifts of head-direction tuning across locations. Our metric summarizes conditional response variation in a common measure for comparing neurons and systems. It can also be applied to joint response maps estimated by an encoding model.

Our analysis reveals complementary selectivity and invariance in the biological populations: CA1 place cells are selective to location and largely invariant to head direction, whereas postsubicular head direction cells are selective to head direction and largely invariant to location. There is some nuance to this invariance (the firing rates of some neurons vary across headings while their place fields remain spatially localized), but the preservation of spatial tuning can support consistent downstream readout, allowing downstream circuits to extract location without changing their readout weights with the animal's head direction.

Applying our approach to deep network models based on vision tasks~\cite{Luo2024,Gornet2024automated}, we find that the tuning properties of individual units differ drastically from those of place cells and head direction cells in the rodent brain.
Individual model units encode entangled representations of head direction and location. Most model units do not exhibit clear marginal tuning to head direction, yet their responses are strongly modulated by head direction through its interaction with location. These results hold for both types of deep network models~\cite{Luo2024,Gornet2024automated}.
One interpretation is that these units are selective to visual features, and apparent spatial selectivity emerges because the visual features in the environment depend on location and head direction jointly.
Even a unit in an early layer of the network that behaves as an edge detector can exhibit location and HD tuning. Such selectivity is substantially different from that of head direction cells and place cells: the network units are much less invariant to head direction and location than place cells and head direction cells in rodents.
These results reveal a major gap in current deep neural network models trained on vision tasks as accounts of the tuning properties of the rodent spatial navigation system. An open question is what additional ingredients would make model units selective to location and invariant to head direction, as in rodents.

Resolution should be chosen from joint coverage and recovery of known responses under the recorded trajectories, with neighboring resolutions reported as sensitivity analyses. A bin width appropriate for a marginal spatial map need not provide adequate sampling after conditioning on heading. Quantitative evaluation of spatial rate-map algorithms likewise shows that binning, smoothing, and sampling must be considered together~\cite{Grieves2023ratemaps}. Binning and support constrain this comparison: the direction of the biological preferences persists across the tested grids, but the HD-cell preference weakens at finer spatial resolution. The scores therefore characterize response invariance at the spatial and angular resolution supported by the recordings.

Our approach can characterize joint tuning to a small number of stimulus or behavioral variables without specifying a functional form for their interactions. Applied to head direction cells, it reveals much stronger dependence on HD than MD. This weak movement-direction dependence suggests that the MD signal in these cells is unlikely to be sufficient for accurate path integration~\cite{Mcnaughton2006}.
The same technique can address the selectivity of place cells on linear tracks to location, time, and distance traveled. Hippocampal neurons on linear tracks have been proposed to encode location, time, distance traveled, or combinations of these variables~\cite{Eichenbaum2014time,Chen2024}. Their strong correlations during behavior make these possibilities difficult to distinguish. Our approach provides a way to test whether tuning to one variable is preserved as the others change, provided the relevant combinations are sampled.

Our results also bear on the study of mixed selectivity~\cite{Rigotti2013}, which has been reported in many brain areas and tasks. Methodologically, our approach quantifies mixed selectivity through the joint tuning and the contribution of each stimulus variable. Our analysis of the deep network models further illustrates how selectivity to visual features can appear as mixed selectivity for spatial variables. In the deep network models examined here, many units in the first layer are oriented filters that encode the orientation of edges.
Analyzed in terms of spatial variables, these units exhibit complex nonlinear mixed selectivity, because the mapping between the visual feature and the spatial variables (location and head direction) is highly nonlinear: an edge of a given orientation appears in the receptive field of the unit only for particular combinations of location and head direction. Mixed selectivity in neural systems has been proposed to carry functional advantages for coding~\cite{Rigotti2013,Fusi2016neurons}. This example highlights the importance of identifying the features driving a response when interpreting the functional significance of mixed selectivity.

This paper focuses on the response properties of individual neurons. An important question is what the observed selectivity and invariance imply at the population level. The tuning properties of individual neurons constrain population-level properties because they define the projection of the neural manifold onto the individual neuronal axes~\cite{Kriegeskorte2021}, but this relationship has mainly been studied for a single stimulus variable. Understanding the relationship between the joint tuning to multiple variables and the structure of the neural manifold is an important direction for future research.

\subsection{Analysis of marginal tuning curves under correlated stimulus sampling}
Here we show how correlated sampling of the stimuli produces spurious tuning in the standard analysis of tuning curves. Consider the tuning of a neuron to variables $x$ and $y$, with joint tuning $h(x,y)$.

Consider a neuron whose joint tuning factorizes as $h(x,y)=f(x)g(y)$. The marginal tuning curve for $x$ estimated by the standard averaging procedure is
$$
\hat{f}(x) = \int p(y|x)h(x,y)dy = \int p(y|x)f(x) g(y)dy =f(x)\int p(y|x)g(y)dy .
$$
If $x$ and $y$ are independent, the procedure recovers $f(x)\int p(y)g(y)dy$, which is proportional to $f(x)$. Otherwise, through the $p(y|x)$ term, the estimated marginal tuning curve for $x$ depends on $g(y)$.

Consider the special case where the neuron is invariant to $x$, i.e., $h(x,y)=h(y)$ is a narrow Gaussian function, so that the true tuning curve for $x$ is constant. If only $y=x$ is sampled (perfectly correlated sampling of the two stimuli), the estimated tuning curve is
$$
\hat{f}(x) = \int p(y|x)h(y)dy = \int \delta(y-x)h(y)dy=h(x).
$$
Relying on the marginal tuning curves, we would incorrectly conclude that the neuron is strongly tuned to $x$.

\subsubsection*{Simulation parameters for the marginal confound and GLM demonstrations (Figs.~\ref{fig:fig1} and \ref{fig:glm})}

For the marginal confound demonstration (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}), $h(x,y)=\exp[-x^2/(2\sigma^2)]$ with $\sigma=0.7$, and $(X,Y)$ is bivariate standard normal with correlation $\rho\in\{0,0.6,0.95\}$. The plotted marginal is the analytic conditional expectation
\[
E[h(X,Y)\mid Y=y]=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+1-\rho^2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\rho^2y^2}{2(\sigma^2+1-\rho^2)}\right].
\]
For the GLM demonstration, $\lambda(x,y)=5\exp[-(x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta)^2/(2\cdot1.2^2)]$ with $\theta=60\degree$ or $45\degree$. Both models use a Poisson observation distribution and a log link. The additive model has $\log\lambda(x,y)=c+u(x)+v(y)$, where $u$ and $v$ are quadratic functions; the interaction model additionally contains an $xy$ term. All coefficients are fitted separately for each model using 20{,}000 observations at independent standard-normal stimuli, and predictions are evaluated on 20{,}000 new observations. Marginals integrate over the actual standard-normal distribution using Gaussian quadrature, checked against the analytic integral. Conditional slices fix $y=1$.

\subsection{Estimating conditional response variance}
We quantify the contribution of each variable by measuring the response variation that remains when the other variables are held fixed. For biological data, observations in each joint bin are randomly split into two equal folds and averaged to give maps $f^{(0)}$ and $f^{(1)}$. The target on a complete uniformly weighted grid is
\begin{equation}
V_x=\mathbb{E}_y\operatorname{Var}_x[f(x,y)],\qquad V_y=\mathbb{E}_x\operatorname{Var}_y[f(x,y)].
\label{eq:1}
\end{equation}
We estimate $V_x$ by centering each fold over $x$ at each fixed $y$, multiplying across folds, and averaging:
\begin{equation}
\widehat V_x=\left\langle(f^{(0)}-\bar f^{(0)}_x)(f^{(1)}-\bar f^{(1)}_x)\right\rangle,\quad
\mathrm{EV}(x)=\frac{\widehat V_x}{\widehat T},\quad
\widehat T=\left\langle(f^{(0)}-\bar f^{(0)})(f^{(1)}-\bar f^{(1)})\right\rangle.
\label{eq:2}
\end{equation}
The bars with subscript $x$ average over $x$ while holding $y$ fixed; bars without a subscript are grand means. The same $\widehat T$ normalizes both axes. Averaging rather than summing removes automatic scaling with the number of bins. Unnormalized biological scores have units of Hz$^2$; normalized scores are dimensionless.

When both folds use the same retained bins and averaging weights, held fixed with respect to firing-rate estimation noise, unbiased fold-map estimates with independent zero-mean errors give $\mathbb{E}[\widehat V_x]=V_x$: the cross-fold noise terms vanish in expectation. This guarantee concerns the unnormalized numerator and the map being estimated. It does not imply that a ratio with an estimated denominator is unbiased. Negative finite-sample estimates are retained. Ratios are undefined when $\widehat T$ is nonpositive or smaller than $10^{-12}$ times the larger within-fold map variance. Undefined ratios are excluded from score comparisons but not from neuron-classification denominators. A sensitivity analysis requires total-map cross-fold correlation greater than $0.1$; this is a stability check rather than a confidence interval.

For complete product-weighted sampling, decomposing signal variance into $x$ main effect, $y$ main effect, and interaction gives $V_x=T_x+T_{xy}$ and $V_y=T_y+T_{xy}$. Thus interactions contribute to both scores, which need not sum to one. The equality line compares conditional response dependence, not exclusive attribution of variance. For incomplete maps, all averages use uniform weights over the joint bins retained in both folds; conditional means use the corresponding supported bins. No unsampled combinations are filled for scoring, and the product-grid decomposition is not assumed on this restricted domain.

Fold maps are smoothed separately with NaN-aware Gaussian kernels, with circular boundaries on angular axes. Grid sizes and interpolation procedures are specified below for each dataset. The primary HD-cell analysis uses a native $10\times10\times90$ grid; finer-grid and interpolated analyses assess sensitivity to these choices. Biological bins at or below the tenth percentile of positive occupancy are excluded; the ANN maps have complete occupancy and no percentile exclusion. Smoothing and interpolation change the target map. In particular, noise correction does not remove systematic within-bin sampling differences, and no unbiasedness claim is made for recovery of the unsmoothed continuous response. Biological temporal dependence can also violate independent-fold errors.

\subsubsection*{Estimator simulations}
The main validation uses Gaussian-ridge rates $\lambda(x,y)=\exp[\log5+(\log35-\log5)\exp(-u^2/(2\cdot0.1^2))]$, with $u=(x-0.4)\cos\theta-(y-0.4)\sin\theta$, evaluated at the centers of a $15\times15$ grid for $\theta=0\degree,25\degree,45\degree$. Each fold contains four independent unit-duration Poisson observations per bin. We generate 600 datasets without smoothing. The numerator is divided by the known noiseless total variance to isolate its estimation properties. A separate sample-count simulation uses $\lambda(x)=5+25\exp[-(x-0.4)^2/(2\cdot0.12^2)]$ and 1--64 observations per bin per fold, with 400 datasets per setting.

The binning simulation uses the same one-dimensional Gaussian response on a $20\times20$ grid. In each joint bin, within-bin $x$ values occupy a narrow interval of width $0.2$ spatial bins, whose center has a fixed independently drawn displacement between $-0.35$ and $+0.35$ bins. Thus the sampled sub-bin locations vary with $y$ despite the true response being independent of $y$. The expected bin rate is evaluated using 100 quadrature points. Each fold averages 20 Poisson observations per joint bin, repeated for 600 datasets. A discrete control evaluates the rate at the spatial-bin center. Gaussian smoothing uses s.d. 1 bin on both axes. These estimates are divided by the known continuous signal variance; dashed references are the respective noiseless binned-map targets. This controlled demonstration separates sub-bin sampling artifacts from noise and denominator estimation.

\subsection{Neural network models}

We evaluated two types of deep networks trained on vision tasks: convolutional neural networks (CNNs) pretrained on image classification, following~\cite{Luo2024}, and a predictive coding network trained to predict the next image frame from the preceding image sequence during navigation~\cite{Gornet2024automated}. Both were analyzed in the same Minecraft environment.

{\bf Environment and images}
The evaluation environment is a $17\times17$ block area with distinctive boundary features. We used this simple square environment instead of the more complex environment of Gornet and Thomson~\cite{Gornet2024automated} to facilitate comparison with the image-classification models studied by Luo et al.~\cite{Luo2024}. Images were rendered in the original Minecraft 1.11.2 world with Malmo 0.37.0. At each of 289 block-center positions, a panorama bank contains 90 headings ($4\degree$ increments) at pitches $-3,-1,0,+1,+3\degree$: 130{,}050 RGB images in total. All models use this same bank. The world files were checked against the training world. The $0\degree$ images define classification and visualization maps; the other pitches define evaluation folds.

{\bf CNNs}
We analyzed an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50~\cite{He2016} (\texttt{layer1}, \texttt{layer3}, \texttt{layer4} and \texttt{avgpool}), the \texttt{avgpool} layer of a randomly initialized ResNet-50, and layer \texttt{features.9} of an ImageNet-pretrained VGG-16~\cite{Simonyan2015}, with the torchvision weights. None of the networks were trained further on the environment. Images were resized and center-cropped to $224\times224$ pixels and normalized with the ImageNet channel means and standard deviations.

{\bf Predictive coding network}
We implemented the architecture of Gornet and Thomson~\cite{Gornet2024automated}, with a convolutional encoder--decoder and two causal attention blocks in the latent space. We collected 500 episodes of 100 RGB frames ($64\times64$ pixels), divided by episode into 400 training, 50 validation, and 50 test episodes. The camera moves at 1.5 blocks/s in fixed 0.1-s simulation steps, with smooth stochastic turns and the agent's head direction matching its direction of movement; positions are set kinematically rather than relying on wall-clock walking physics. Training uses 4{,}000 nonoverlapping ten-frame chunks, with the first nine predictions supervised by the next nine frames within each chunk.

We follow the released training notebook: two attention heads, batches of 64 sequences, 200 epochs (12{,}600 updates) of SGD, weight decay $5\times10^{-6}$, and OneCycle maximum learning rate 0.1. The optimizer is initialized with momentum 0.9 and OneCycle cycles momentum between 0.95 and 0.85; Nesterov momentum is not enabled. We retain the released fixed RGB normalization and mean-squared-error objective. The selected checkpoint is epoch 89, chosen by full-validation mean-squared error; test episodes are not used for selection. Validation and test errors are averaged over all evaluated predictions. The released validation routine instead evaluates a limited number of batches and divides by its configured batch limit rather than the number actually evaluated.

We correct temporal leakage in normalization. The released latent normalization pools over time as well as channels and space, so future frames can influence earlier representations despite causal attention. Its batch normalization also couples frames during training. We instead use per-frame layer normalization over channels and space in latent blocks and single-group group normalization in convolutional blocks. Future-frame perturbations leave earlier outputs unchanged in training and evaluation modes. Held-out MSE is 0.091739 for images normalized using the released fixed RGB statistics. In unnormalized $[0,1]$ RGB units, pixel MSE is 0.004845, versus 0.016137 for copying the current frame and 0.010370 when the trained model receives a repeated current frame instead of the real history.

We analyzed four layers: the first convolutional layer (\texttt{conv1}) and first downsampling block (\texttt{down1}) of the encoder, the encoder output before the attention blocks (pre-prediction latent), and the output of the second attention and feed-forward block (post-prediction latent). To obtain the response to a single view, each image was repeated as a sequence of 10 identical frames and the latent at the last time step was taken.

{\bf Unit responses and EV}
For layers with more than 8{,}000 units, a fixed random subset of 8{,}000 units was analyzed. Responses to the $0\degree$-pitch panoramas define each unit's location$\times$HD map ($289\times90$) used for cell classification. Responses to the pitch panoramas were used for $\mathrm{EV}$: the two folds are the mean responses over pitches $\{-3,+1\}\degree$ and $\{-1,+3\}\degree$, so that the fold maps use disjoint image sets at each state. These folds measure robustness across pitch perturbations, not repeated stochastic measurement noise. The fold maps were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (s.d. 1 bin on each true spatial axis and on circular head direction), and the head-direction axis was upsampled by a factor of 5 before computing $\mathrm{EV}_{xy}$ and $\mathrm{EV}_{hd}$.

{\bf Place cell classification}
We identified putative place fields using the iterative field-detection algorithm of Tanni et al.~\cite{Tanni2022}, with size and peak criteria for ANN activation maps. Classification used marginal spatial maps; conditional response dependence was assessed separately with EV. The location$\times$HD map of each unit was rescaled to $[0,1]$ and summed over head directions. Starting at the mean of this location map, the detection threshold was raised in steps of $0.1$ times its spatial standard deviation, tracking contiguous fields and their subfields. Candidate fields have four-neighbor connectivity, at least 15 bins, and a peak at least half the maximum of the map. Fields must cover at most 144 of the 289 location bins. Within each nested sequence, the lowest-threshold candidate satisfying the criteria is retained. A valid parent replaces a single retained child; when multiple child fields are retained, they remain separate and the parent is discarded. A unit with at least one retained field meets the place criterion. The same procedure and thresholds were applied to all ten analyzed model layers. Temporal stability was not assessed in this static-image evaluation. Heading-group stability was evaluated separately as a supplementary selection criterion (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ann_stability}).

For classification, post-prediction activations were rectified at zero; the original signed activations were retained for EV and visualization. Units with positive heading-summed responses in fewer than 1\% of locations were classified as inactive. Border score $>0.5$ and directional score $>0.47$ define the other two criteria. Main pies combine the union of place and border labels into location selectivity, retaining location-plus-HD, HD-only, other-active, and inactive categories. The supplement retains all intersections of the original criteria. Red scatter points and place-only analyses use the exclusive place category (neither border nor HD). The pyramidal waveform criterion of~\cite{Tanni2022} has no analogue for model units and is not used.



\subsection{Neural data}

{\bf CA1 recordings}
We analyzed the recordings of Pfeiffer and Foster~\cite{Pfeiffer2013}. Four adult male Long-Evans rats performed an open-field spatial memory task in a $2 \times 2$~m black arena enclosed by 30~cm walls, with 36 identical, evenly spaced reward wells flush with the floor. On each trial, after consuming a reward at a randomly selected well, the rat navigated back to a fixed home well for a second reward; the home well changed each day. Each session contained about 30 trials, giving broad coverage of the arena. Wells were filled silently via tubing beneath the floor, and no explicit cue signaled which well was filled.
Each rat was implanted with a microdrive array targeting dorsal CA1. Units were identified by manual clustering of spike waveform amplitudes; only well-isolated units were included, and putative interneurons were excluded by spike width and mean rate. Position and head direction were acquired with two head-mounted LEDs and an overhead camera; the supplied tracking arrays used here have a median interval of approximately 33.4~ms. The supplied first open-field session of each rat contains $551$ putative excitatory units in total. We applied the published place-cell criterion~\cite{Pfeiffer2013}, retaining units with a peak spatial firing rate above $1$~Hz during movement faster than $5$~cm/s. Selection maps use $2$-cm spatial bins and Gaussian smoothing with s.d. $4$~cm. We accumulated spike counts and dwell times using the actual tracking intervals, interpolated position at each spike, and excluded intervals longer than $1.5$ times the median tracking interval. Smoothed spike counts were divided by smoothed dwell times, and peaks were evaluated in bins visited during eligible movement. The criterion retained $204$, $170$, $79$, and $92$ cells from rats 1--4, respectively ($545$ total). Selection used the complete session; decoder tuning maps were then estimated on training folds and evaluated on held-out folds. We retained all supplied excitatory cells as a robustness analysis (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ca1_selection}). The fine selection maps are distinct from the coarser joint location$\times$HD maps used to estimate conditional variance.

Spikes were binned into 250~ms time bins. Each spike bin is assigned the nearest tracking sample to its center, with no extrapolation outside tracking coverage or across gaps larger than 1.5 median tracking intervals. Duplicate tracking timestamps are merged using linear means for position and circular means for heading. Each spike bin contributes once to a joint state. Location was binned into $20\times 20$ bins (10~cm) and head direction into 60 bins ($6\degree$). Time bins in which the animal moved slower than 5~cm/s were excluded, and location$\times$HD bins with fewer than 5 time bins were discarded. The fold maps were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (s.d. 1 bin along location and 2 bins along head direction), and the head-direction axis was resampled to match the 400 location bins.

For decoding, we used a memoryless Bayesian decoder with independent Poisson neurons and a uniform prior. For each state bin $s$ (a head-direction bin or a 2-D location bin), the tuning $f_i(s)$ of each neuron was estimated from occupancy-normalized firing rates. Given spike counts $\mathbf{n}_t = (n_{t,1}, \dots, n_{t, N})$ in a time window of duration $\tau$, the posterior is
\begin{equation}
P(s \mid \mathbf{n}_t) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(s)}{\sum_{s'=1}^{M} \mathcal{L}(s')},
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the number of state bins and the Poisson likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(s) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{N} \left[f_i(s)\,\tau\right]^{n_{t,i}}\right) \exp\!\left(-\tau \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i(s)\right).
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
The decoded state is the posterior maximum over supported states; states with no finite training-rate estimates are excluded. For the place cells we used $\tau=250$~ms windows, excluded time bins with speed below 5~cm/s, binned head direction into $1\degree$ bins (tuning curves smoothed with a circular Gaussian of s.d. $2\degree$) and location into $2$~cm bins (tuning maps smoothed with a Gaussian of s.d. $4$~cm). We used 4-fold cross-validation over contiguous blocks of time: tuning curves were estimated from three folds and the held-out fold was decoded. The occupancy-based baseline always reports the most occupied state bin of the training folds. Errors are absolute circular angular differences for head direction and Euclidean distances for location. We report medians pooled over eligible held-out time bins and display individual session medians separately; pooled summaries weight sessions by their number of eligible bins.

{\bf Head direction cells}
We analyzed head direction cells from Duszkiewicz et al.~\cite{Duszkiewicz2024}. Mice foraged for small food rewards in an open field: a modular arena ($80 \times 80$~cm floor, 50~cm high walls) on a plastic platform inside a larger metal frame ($90 \times 90 \times 180$~cm), with a white cue card on one wall as the main distal landmark. Neural activity was recorded from the postsubiculum with 64-channel silicon probes. Units were classified as putative excitatory or fast-spiking inhibitory cells from waveform shape and mean firing rate, and excitatory cells whose head-direction information exceeded the 99th percentile of a time-reversed control distribution were labeled head direction cells. Position and head orientation were tracked at 100~Hz. We analyzed the 30 sessions of Datasets 1 and 2 of the release that were recorded in the square arena, and all cells labeled both excitatory and head-direction cells ($n=1{,}489$).

We use the curated NWB tracking release, with position in centimeters and heading clockwise from $+Y$. Spike bins are 50~ms, assigned to tracking at their centers as above. Speed is the norm of the centered XY derivative, smoothed with a Gaussian of s.d. three tracking samples; tracking gaps are excluded. The primary HD-cell analysis uses $10\times10$ location bins spanning each session's observed range, chosen using joint coverage and recovery of known responses replayed on the recorded trajectories; HD uses 90 bins ($4\degree$). Median spatial bin widths are approximately 8.6 and 8.7~cm along the two axes. The finer $20\times20$ and $30\times30$ grids remain sensitivity analyses. The location$\times$HD occupancy floor is five spike bins and the speed threshold is 2~cm/s. Fold maps are smoothed on the two spatial axes with s.d. 0.5 bin and on circular HD with s.d. one bin. These kernels correspond to approximately 4.3~cm spatially and $4\degree$ angularly. Primary HD-cell scores use the native grid without interpolation. An interpolated $20\times20$ analysis is included as a sensitivity check. CA1 retains its $20\times20$ grid in the 201-cm arena, approximately 10~cm per bin; equal numbers of bins would not imply equal physical resolution across these datasets.

For HD$\times$MD, the primary analysis uses $36\times36$ bins ($10\degree$), a floor of ten spike bins, and speed at least 5~cm/s. The $90\times90$ grid is a sensitivity analysis; angular smoothing has s.d. $4\degree$ in both cases. We estimate velocity by fitting XY position against actual tracking timestamps within a centered 200-ms window (21 samples at 100~Hz). Speed and MD are the norm and angle of this same velocity vector, with MD expressed clockwise from $+Y$, matching HD. Windows must be complete and cannot cross missing coordinates or timestamp gaps greater than 1.5 times the median tracking interval; stationary direction is undefined. This centered estimate is an offline behavioral measurement, not a causal readout. HD and MD are paired at zero lag. We selected the 200-ms window and 5~cm/s threshold before examining the new results and evaluated 100/400-ms windows and 1/2/10~cm/s thresholds. HD and MD decoders use identical eligible and jointly decodable time bins, with the same four contiguous time folds.

Decoding uses four contiguous held-out folds for all 30 sessions, with 50-ms windows. Location/HD decoding uses speed at least 2~cm/s; the matched HD/MD analysis uses the velocity estimate and 5~cm/s threshold specified above. Counts are Gaussian-smoothed within each held-out block (s.d. 100~ms). HD and MD use $1\degree$ bins with circular smoothing of s.d. $3\degree$; location uses $32\times32$ bins over the observed session range. Spatial training bins with fewer than ten eligible spike bins are unsupported and excluded. The baseline uses the training-occupancy mode on the same state grid.

\subsection*{Predictive-model normalization and prediction performance}

We changed the released implementation's internal normalization to preserve causal prediction. Its latent normalization pools statistics over time as well as channels and space, allowing future frames to affect earlier representations despite the causal attention mask. Batch normalization in the convolutional blocks also couples frames during training. We replaced these operations with per-frame layer normalization in the latent blocks and single-group group normalization in the convolutional blocks, and trained the model from scratch. Perturbing future frames left earlier outputs unchanged in both training and evaluation modes. The fixed RGB input normalization was retained; this is distinct from the internal normalization correction.

The retained checkpoint was selected at epoch 89 by mean-squared error over the complete validation set. On the held-out test episodes, its next-frame MSE was $0.091739$ on normalized images and $0.004845$ in $[0,1]$ RGB units, compared with $0.016137$ in RGB units for copying the current frame. Our normalized-image MSE is numerically similar to the image-prediction MSE of $0.094$ reported by Gornet and Thomson~\cite{Gornet2024automated}. As an additional control, supplying repeated copies of the current frame to our trained model instead of the true history increased RGB MSE to $0.010370$, showing that prediction benefits from temporal history.

\subsection*{Sensitivity to sampling and interpolation}
The primary HD-cell analysis uses a native location$\times$HD grid, with HD exceeding location in $96.9\%$ of cells. On the finer native $20\times20$ spatial grid, this fraction is $86.0\%$; the interpolated $20\times20$ analysis gives $87.8\%$. On the native CA1 grid, location exceeds HD in $94.4\%$ of $522$ defined ratios. With finer spike bins, the CA1 preference is $93.8\%$ of $503$ defined ratios. Requiring total-map cross-fold correlation greater than $0.1$ retains $505$ CA1 cells, of which $98.6\%$ favor location; the paired medians are $0.74$ and $0.08$. Thus the preference is not driven by the extreme ratios from small denominators.

Across all twelve combinations of 100/200/400-ms movement-estimation windows and 1/2/5/10~cm/s speed thresholds, HD scores exceed MD scores in 98.3--100\% of cells with defined estimates (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:md_sensitivity}). At 200~ms, MD decoding error decreases from $61.2\degree$ at 1~cm/s to $36.3\degree$ at 10~cm/s; corresponding HD errors remain $8.3$--$8.7\degree$. At 5~cm/s, MD errors are $52.5\degree$, $48.4\degree$, and $40.8\degree$ for 100-, 200-, and 400-ms windows. These changes reflect both the movement periods retained and the temporal scale of the target variable. The tracking-derived MD remains an estimate of the tracked point's motion rather than an independent measurement of body velocity.

In the finer $90\times90$ HD$\times$MD grid, $1289$ of $1489$ cells have defined normalized scores; $87.6\%$ favor HD (median scores $0.142$ for HD and $0.004$ for MD). The $36\times36$ primary grid retains all cells, with corresponding medians $0.904$ and $0.050$. Fine and coarse grids retain different supported domains; sparse conditional support can itself reduce estimated variation. Their score magnitudes therefore do not establish greater invariance at finer resolution.

\subsection*{Within-bin sampling and smoothing}
A continuous response can be invariant to $y$ while its binned map appears $y$-dependent if different $y$ bins sample different sub-bin values of $x$. Cross-fold noise correction targets that binned map and cannot remove this systematic discrepancy. Smoothing mitigates the artifact in the example below, but can also attenuate genuine response structure. Interpolation does not add observations. These considerations motivate reporting binning and smoothing choices and native-grid sensitivity rather than treating smoothing as a universal bias correction.

\subsection*{Joint coverage and trajectory-based recovery}
The HD-cell primary grid was chosen using joint coverage and trajectory-based recovery. For $10\times10$, $20\times20$, and $30\times30$ spatial grids with $4\degree$ heading bins, the median session retains 20.2\%, 3.6\%, and 1.2\% of possible joint bins after occupancy filtering. The median number of retained heading bins per retained location (summarized across sessions) is approximately 16, 3, and 2. Consequently, finer grids can restrict the headings compared within each location and reduce the measured HD variation without a change in the underlying response.

We replayed six known continuous rate functions along each of the 30 recorded trajectories: three HD-only functions (two broad and one narrow), two spatial Gaussian fields, and a spatially gain-modulated HD function. Rates range from 1 to 20~Hz; broad/narrow HD functions use $\exp[\kappa(\cos(\theta-\phi)-1)]$ with $\kappa=2/8$, and spatial fields have s.d. 15\% of the arena extent along each axis. Eight independent Poisson realizations per function and session used the original 50-ms time bins, alignment, speed selection, split folds, occupancy filtering, and physical smoothing bandwidth. Each noisy estimate was compared with the corresponding noiseless fold replay and with an unsmoothed complete-grid reference (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectory_recovery}). On the $10\times10$ grid, all 720 HD-only and 480 place-only estimates favored their correct variable. Median absolute errors relative to the noiseless replay were below 0.012 on either axis for each pure-response function. The mixed response was also recovered relative to its noiseless replay, but its balance changed across support domains. These controls validate recovery of the processed, sampled target for the tested functions; they do not establish unbiased recovery of arbitrary continuous responses or identify the biological origin of all residual modulation.

These results motivate choosing spatial and angular resolution jointly using coverage and trajectory-based recovery, then reporting neighboring resolutions. Neither a conventional marginal-map bin width nor a strong separation between cell populations is by itself a sufficient selection criterion~\cite{Grieves2023ratemaps}.
